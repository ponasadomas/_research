# US first-time mothers of babies under two: published figures

This page collects public figures from official and research sources to stand beside the forum quotes, which only show people who post online. It was gathered on 2026-11-17 by an agent using web search and page download. Every figure is a national average for a wider group than our buyer (often all mothers, or all parents of children under 3, 5 or 6), not a measurement of first-time mothers of babies 0 to 24 months. The years differ from figure to figure, and each line says which year and which group. Every number below is copied word for word into a file in `raw/` named after the source.

## 1. Her situation

- Mean age of mother at first birth: 27.6 years, a record high (27.5 in 2023; 25.4 in 2010). Year 2024. Covers mothers of first-born children, all US births. Source: NCHS, National Vital Statistics Reports vol. 75 no. 2, "Births: Final Data for 2024" (Table 4 and text). https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data/nvsr/nvsr75/nvsr75-02.pdf (raw: `nchs-births-final-data-2024.txt`)
- First births: 1,427,020 first children among 3,628,934 births. Year 2024. All US births by live-birth order. Source: same NVSR, Table 3. (The share is not printed as a percent in the report, so none is given here.) (raw: `nchs-births-final-data-2024.txt`)
- Births to unmarried mothers: 39.5%. Year 2024. All births, not first births only. Source: same NVSR, Table A. (raw: `nchs-births-final-data-2024.txt`)
- Education of mother: 88.6% high school diploma or higher; 36.7% bachelor's degree or higher. Year 2024. All births, not first births only. Source: same NVSR, Table A. (raw: `nchs-births-final-data-2024.txt`)
- Median household income of "someone with a birth in 2021": $85,320. Year 2021 (births January 2020 to December 2021). Women aged 15 to 50 who reported a birth in the last 12 months; any birth order. Source: US Census Bureau, working paper poster SEHSD-WP2023-12, American Community Survey 2019 and 2021 1-year files. https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/working-papers/2023/demo/SEHSD-WP2023-12.pdf (raw: `census-recent-births-acs-2021.txt`)

Not found: share of first-time mothers who are married or partnered at birth; share of first-time mothers with a college degree; household income of families with a child under one or under three. (The NVSR does not print marital status or education by birth order; CDC WONDER would, but its query tool was not used.)

## 2. Work

- Labour force participation of mothers with own children under 3: 65.4%. Year 2024 (annual average). Mothers living with own children under 3. Source: BLS, "Employment Characteristics of Families - 2024", Table 6. https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/releases/famee/famee_20250423.pdf (copy of the BLS release of 2025-04-23; bls.gov itself refused download) (raw: `bls-employment-characteristics-of-families-2024.txt`)
- Labour force participation of mothers whose youngest child is under 1 year: 61.9%. Year 2024. Same table. (Same raw file.)
- Labour force participation of mothers with children under age 6: 68.3%, against 78.0% for mothers whose youngest child is 6 to 17. Year 2024. Source: same release, text and Table 5. (Same raw file.)
- Full-time work: 76 percent of employed mothers with children under age 6 worked full time (81 percent of those with children 6 to 17); 79 percent of all employed mothers against 95 percent of employed fathers. Year 2024. Source: same release. (Same raw file.) A figure for mothers of children under 3 only is not printed as a percent.
- Median leave taken by mothers after a birth or adoption: 11 weeks (12 weeks for household income $75,000 or more; 10 weeks for $30,000 to $74,999; six weeks under $30,000); 47% were off 12 weeks or more. Survey of adults 18 to 70 who took or needed leave in the previous two years, published March 2017 (survey about 2016). Covers mothers, any child. Source: Pew Research Center. https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2017/03/23/an-inside-look-at-family-and-medical-leave-in-america-the-experiences-of-those-who-took-leave-and-those-who-needed-or-wanted-to-but-couldnt/ (raw: `pew-family-medical-leave-2017.txt`)
- Leave in 2026: moms who returned to work after taking leave took a median of 10 weeks; 6 percent of moms took two weeks or less; 88 percent of moms returned to work after leave; 15 percent of parents employed when the youngest child was born took no time off. Year 2026 survey, nearly 5,500 parents of children under six, fielded by NORC. Source: New America, 2026 National Parent Survey. https://www.newamerica.org/insights/what-we-learned-about-parental-leave-from-the-2026-national-parent-survey/ (raw: `new-america-parental-leave-2026.txt`)
- Access to paid family leave: 27% of private-industry employees, March 2023 (parental, maternity or family-care leave provided by the employer, separate from sick, vacation or disability leave). Private-sector workers of any family type. Source: Congressional Research Service report R44835 (March 2025), citing the Bureau of Labor Statistics employee benefits survey. https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-03-26_R44835_62915196e88791f816921d464cb3d0b705eff556.html (raw: `crs-paid-family-medical-leave-2025.txt`)

Not found: a typical return-to-work week count from a federal source (the two figures above are from surveys of parents who took leave); a March 2025 or later BLS paid family leave figure (a trade-press article reported a newer BLS number but the BLS release itself could not be fetched, so it is not used); 2025 BLS family employment data (the 2026 release exists but bls.gov blocked download).

## 3. Who looks after the baby

- Children under age 3 in a regular nonparental child care arrangement: 53 percent. Year 2024. All US children under 3; "regular" is the survey's definition. Unpaid care from an individual other than a parent was the most common type (24 percent), followed by center-based care (18 percent). Source: ASPE issue brief, 10 November 2026, citing the OPRE report on the 2024 National Survey of Early Care and Education (Ventura et al., 2026; the OPRE page itself returned 403). https://aspe.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/documents/a8bceacd4847aed313841e37836da40b/Child-Care-Parent-Preferences.pdf (raw: `aspe-child-care-parent-preferences-2026.txt`)
- Grandparents: "Nearly half of children under age five spent time in grandparent care while a parent was working, in school, or otherwise unavailable." Children under 5, any amount of time. Page dated May 7, 2026; the year of the underlying data is not stated in the fetched text. Source: Roosevelt Institute fact sheet (report by Sarah Jane Glynn), using the Census Bureau Survey of Income and Program Participation. Secondary source; the Census tables were not checked. https://rooseveltinstitute.org/publications/fact-sheet-whos-minding-the-kids-these-days/ (raw: `roosevelt-whos-minding-the-kids-sipp.txt`)

Not found: a figure for children under 1 only; a Census Bureau table for grandparent care of infants.

## 4. Her day and her media

- Time on primary childcare: adults living in households with children under age 6 spent an average of 2.5 hours per day; women 3.0 hours, men 2.0 hours. Year 2024. Adults (any sex) with a child under 6 in the household. Source: BLS, American Time Use Survey 2024 results. https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/releases/atus/atus_20250626.pdf (raw: `bls-american-time-use-survey-2024.txt`)
- Leisure: employed adults with a child under age 6 had 3.0 hours of leisure and sports per day, against 4.5 hours for employed adults with no child under 18. Year 2024. Same source. (Same raw file.)
- Sleep: women whose youngest household child is under 6 slept 9.08 hours per day on average (includes naps and spells of sleeplessness). Year 2024. Same source, Table 8A. This is time recorded as sleeping, not sleep quality or night waking. (Same raw file.)
- Social media for parenting advice: four in five parents of a child aged 0 to 4 use social media for parenting advice or to share experiences; most mothers do. Two in five parents think it is difficult to distinguish good from bad advice there; nearly half rate it very useful for new ideas. 614 parents with at least one child aged 0 to 4, poll released November 2023 (date taken from a news-site URL; the fetched text has no date). Source: University of Michigan Health C.S. Mott Children's Hospital National Poll on Children's Health, press release on EurekAlert. https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1008132 (raw: `mott-poll-social-media-parenting-advice-2023.txt`)
- Smartphone ownership: 91% of US women and 97% of adults aged 30 to 49 own a smartphone. Year 2026 (survey Jan. 30 to June 18, 2026). All adults, not mothers. Source: Pew Research Center mobile fact sheet. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/mobile/ (raw: `pew-mobile-fact-sheet-2026.txt`)
- Babies and screens: parents say 82% of children under 2 ever watch TV; about four in ten say their child under 2 ever uses or interacts with a smartphone; 62% of parents of a child under 2 say the child watches YouTube (up from 45%, 2020). Year 2025, parents of children 12 and younger. Source: Pew Research Center, "How Parents Manage Screen Time for Kids", 8 October 2025. https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/10/08/how-parents-manage-screen-time-for-kids/ (raw: `pew-how-parents-manage-screen-time-2025.txt`)

Not found: how much new mothers trust each source (pediatrician, family, internet, social media) on a common scale; where first-time mothers specifically get information; use of parenting or baby-tracking apps among US mothers (only a regional Louisiana survey and a 2014 industry survey turned up, not used); mothers' own social media use (the Pew social media fact sheet was not fetched).

## 5. Development and worry

- Parents who suspected their child might be delayed in meeting milestones: nearly a quarter. Of those, 63% asked a healthcare provider, 24% a childcare provider, and 18% only looked up information online, asked family or friends or sought advice on social media. 779 parents with at least one child five or under; year 2021 (from a news-site URL; the fetched text has no date). Source: Mott Poll press release on EurekAlert. https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/600960 (raw: `mott-poll-milestone-worry-2021.txt`)
- Parents with some level of concern about their child's learning, development or behavior: an average 55% across 2025, about a quarter very concerned; 64% very or somewhat concerned by November 2025. Households with a child under age three, 5,816 responses, 2025. Source: ZERO TO THREE analysis of the Stanford Center on Early Childhood RAPID Survey (an online panel, not a random sample). https://www.zerotothree.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/2026_baby_RAPID_brief_FINAL.pdf (raw: `zerotothree-rapid-baby-brief-2025.txt`)
- Developmental screening: 36.5% of children aged 9 to 35 months had a parent complete a standardized developmental screening tool in the past 12 months. Years 2023-2024 (two-year estimate). Source: America's Health Rankings, using the HRSA Maternal and Child Health Bureau National Survey of Children's Health. https://www.americashealthrankings.org/explore/measures/devscreen_overall (raw: `ahr-developmental-screening-nsch-2023-2024.txt`)
- Well-child visits: 79.6% of children aged 0 to 17 had one or more preventive visits in the past 12 months; the page says this is higher for ages 0 to 2 than 3 to 17 but gives no figure for 0 to 2. Years 2023-2024. Same type of source (National Survey of Children's Health via America's Health Rankings). https://www.americashealthrankings.org/explore/measures/wellchild_visit_0to17_overall (raw: `ahr-well-child-visit-nsch-2023-2024.txt`)

Not found: how many parents use the CDC milestone checklists or the CDC Milestone Tracker app (no published figure); a national percent of well-child visit attendance for babies 0 to 24 months; a current NSCH percentage of parents worried about development (only the sources above).

## 6. Spending

- Estimated annual expenditure on a child aged 0 to 2 by married-couple families: $12,680 (before-tax income $59,200 to $107,400), of which child care and education $2,870; $9,690 for income under $59,200; $19,770 for income over $107,400. Year 2015, in 2015 dollars (report published January 2017). Per year for the 0 to 2 age band, not a first-year total. Source: USDA Center for Nutrition Policy and Promotion, "Expenditures on Children by Families, 2015", Table 1. https://fns-prod.azureedge.us/sites/default/files/expenditures_on_children_by_families/crc2015.pdf (raw: `usda-expenditures-on-children-2015.txt`)

Not found: spending in the baby's first year as a separate figure from a primary source; spending on apps, books, classes or toys for development.

## What this adds to the forum quotes

- She is older than the stereotype: the average first-time mother in 2024 was 27.6 and the age keeps rising, so many are mid-career, not teenagers.
- Most mothers of young children are still in paid work: 61.9% of mothers of a baby under 1 and 65.4% of mothers of a child under 3 were in the labour force in 2024. "What do I do with the baby all day" is often asked by a mother who also has a job and a child-care plan.
- Leave is short and mostly unpaid: median leaves of 10 and 11 weeks in two surveys, and 27% of private-sector workers had employer paid family leave in 2023. This sits behind the forum posts about going back to work and feeling the baby is not getting enough.
- The baby is often with someone else part of the week: 53 percent of under-3s are in regular nonparental care, and relatives (including grandparents) are the largest single type. This fits the quotes where grandparents compare or advise.
- Worry about development is common and so is looking online: nearly a quarter of parents of children five or under suspected a delay, 18% of those only asked online, family or friends, and four in five parents of 0 to 4s use social media for parenting advice. Only 36.5% of 9 to 35 month olds got a standardized screening tool.
- Her time is taken: adults with a child under 6 spent 2.5 hours a day on primary childcare, women 3.0 hours, and the babies she looks after are already on screens (82% of under-2s watch TV).

## Cautions

- Population: almost no figure here is about first-time mothers. Mean age and first-birth counts are; everything else covers all mothers, all parents, or all adults with a child under a given age (under 1, 3, 5, 6, or 13).
- Age bands differ: "under 3" and "under 6" include children well past 24 months; "9 to 35 months" and "0 to 2" are the closest matches to our 0 to 24 months, and none matches it exactly.
- Years differ from 2015 (USDA spending) and 2017 (Pew leave) to 2026. The USDA dollars are 2015 dollars. Leave surveys measure different groups and so differ.
- Surveys by Mott (small samples, 614 and 779 parents), ZERO TO THREE/RAPID (online panel) and Pew leave are opinion or panel surveys; their percentages are not federal statistics.
- Four sources are secondary (ASPE, Roosevelt Institute, CRS, America's Health Rankings); each names its primary source and the primary page was not always reachable (BLS and OPRE pages refused download; NCHS, the BLS releases via the St. Louis Fed archive, USDA, Census and Pew were read directly).
- The ATUS sleep figure is hours recorded as sleeping for women with a youngest child under 6, which includes naps; it is not a measure of broken nights.
- The BLS figures are the 2024 release (April 2025). A 2025 release (April 2026) exists but could not be downloaded.
- None of this describes who buys. It describes the national backdrop, not the buyer.
