Earlier this year, the New Practice Lab at New America conducted the 2026 National Parent Survey, which included new, nationally representative data about parents’ use of leave and leave preferences after the birth of a child. 
 More about the survey: The 2026 National Parent Survey, fielded by NORC at the University of Chicago, asked nearly 5,500 parents of children under the age of six about their experiences with parental leave, child care, time with their family, and what they want for their children. Throughout this post, we use the terms “moms,” “dads,” and “parents” for readability. The vast majority of respondents (96 percent) were parents, including biological parents, stepparents, adoptive parents, and foster parents. The remaining 4 percent were other caregivers, including grandparents (2 percent); aunts, uncles, or siblings (1 percent); legal guardians (0.7 percent); and other caregivers (0.3 percent).The survey data is limited to leave after the birth of the respondent’s youngest child, described here as “parental leave.” The survey did not ask about other types of leave, such as leave after adoption or foster placement, medical leave, family caregiving leave, safety leave, or military exigency leave. The survey also did not ask whether the respondent’s leave was paid or unpaid. However, results from the New Practice Lab’s new New York City Parent Survey, funded by the Robin Hood Foundation, provides additional parental leave findings that include whether New York City parents took paid or unpaid leave—and if the latter, why they did not use New York’s paid family leave program. Additional information about the 2026 National Parent Survey, including the survey instrument and public-use data files, is available in the full report.
Among parents who were employed when their youngest child was born, 15 percent reported not taking any time off from work. This means an estimated 3 million (employed) parents nationally took no leave after the birth of a child, nearly 1 million of them mothers. 
While most parents (59 percent) took six weeks of leave or less, there were significant differences in leave duration by gender (see Figure 1). Dads who returned to work after taking leave (96 percent) reported taking far less leave (median of 2 weeks) than moms who returned to work (median of 10 weeks). 
Half of dads (51 percent) took short leaves of two weeks or less, compared to 6 percent of moms. Most moms returned to work after taking leave (88 percent). Leave durations among moms who returned skewed longer than dads: 29 percent took 9 to 12 weeks of leave, and another 26 percent took 13 or more weeks.
