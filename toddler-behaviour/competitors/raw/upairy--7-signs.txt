# Seven Things Toddlers Do When Potty Training Stalls — and the One Cause Behind All of Them

Refusing the potty. Hiding behind the couch to poop. A diaper that stays dry for two hours and gets soaked the second it goes back on. Most parents read these as separate problems, or as a phase. Line them up and they point at one thing — and it isn’t behavior.

Ask a room of parents what potty training looks like when it isn’t working, and you get the same list every time. She won’t sit on it. She runs when it comes out. She disappears behind the couch to poop. She pees mid-play and doesn’t even glance down.

Everyone treats these as separate problems. A stubbornness problem, a fear problem, a timing problem. So parents try a sticker chart for one, a new potty for another, a bribe for the third.

Put them side by side and something else shows up. They aren’t seven problems. They’re seven views of the same one.

Read them that way and the pattern is obvious. Every one of these is a child who can hold it, who understands privacy, who understands the toilet — and who is missing one piece of information.

She isn’t being difficult. She’s being asked to answer a question she never heard.

There’s a name going around for this pattern: delayed potty training. It isn’t a medical diagnosis and nobody should be labeling your child. But as a description of what’s happening, it’s a fair one. The training isn’t failing. It hasn’t started yet, because the part that comes first never happened.

## What she’s actually missing

Learning to use the potty isn’t one skill. It’s a loop, and it runs four steps.

Step 1
Her body signals

A full bladder sends a message. This part works fine, and always has.

Step 2
She goes

No warning, no pause. She hasn’t learned to read step 1 yet.

Step 3 — missing
She feels it happen

Gone in about two seconds. The diaper is built to make sure of it.

Step 4
Her brain links the two

It can’t. There’s nothing to link the signal to.

Steps 1, 2 and 4 are all working. The loop breaks in one place — and it’s the one place nobody thinks to look.

That third step is the whole thing. A child learns her body the same way she learned her hands: she does something, something happens, and after enough repeats the two get wired together. Take away “something happens” and the wiring never forms.

Which is exactly what a modern diaper is designed to do. The gel core pulls wetness off her skin almost instantly. She stays comfortable and keeps playing. That is the feature. It’s what you paid for, and with a newborn it’s precisely what you want.

But she’s not a newborn anymore. And every single time she goes, the lesson gets deleted before she can read it.

Chances to learn, per year

2,900

A toddler pees roughly eight times a day. That’s about 2,900 chances a year for her body to teach her something — and in a modern diaper, close to none of them land.

So when people say she’ll do it when she’s ready, they’re right about one thing and wrong about another.

## Why “wait until she’s ready” can wait forever

Ready isn’t a birthday. Ask anyone what it looks like and you get the same answers: she notices she’s gone, she tells you after, then one day she tells you before.

Every one of those is step 3. They’re signs that the loop closed.

So waiting for her to seem ready means waiting for a signal that’s being switched off around 2,900 times a year. You can wait a very long time for that. Plenty of families do.

Readiness isn’t something she grows into. It’s something she gets told — by her own body.

## First: two of those signs can mean something else

Before going further, this part matters more than anything we could sell you.

Hiding to poop, and screaming when the diaper comes off, are the two on the list that sometimes point somewhere different. If she strains, goes several days between poops, passes hard or very large stools, or you’ve seen a streak of blood on the wipe, that can be constipation, stool withholding, or a small tear that makes going hurt. A child who expects pain will hide, hold, and fight the changing mat — and no underwear on earth fixes that.

Same goes for a sudden reversal in a child who had it and lost it, and for pain or burning when she pees. Those are worth a call to your pediatrician, not a new product.

If none of that sounds like your house, read on.

## What the first few weeks look like

Nothing about this is instant, and any page that promises you three days is lying. What parents describe instead is an order of events. The dates move around. The order doesn’t.

-
Days 1–3: more accidents, not fewer
Two or three a day, caught by the underwear. This is the part that makes you wonder if you’ve made things worse. You haven’t — she’s getting information she’s never had.

-
The first time she notices
She stops mid-play, looks down, and tells you. Usually in one word. That’s step 3 arriving for the first time, and everything else is built on it.

-
Then she tells you after
She comes and finds you instead of carrying on. Accidents haven’t dropped yet. Awareness has.

-
Then she tells you before
Near misses first, then real ones. This is the point where the potty stops being your idea and starts being hers.

#### Nights are a separate thing entirely

Staying dry overnight runs on a hormone, not on training, and it arrives when it arrives — often months or years after daytime. Keep the diaper on at night for as long as she needs it. None of this is about that.

## Parents usually ask

Isn’t she just not ready?

Maybe. But the signs of readiness everyone tells you to wait for — noticing, telling you after, telling you before — all depend on her being able to feel it in the first place. If she can hold it for a couple of hours, the holding part already works. That’s usually the piece people mistake for not being ready.

We already use disposables. Isn’t that the same thing?

They look like the middle step but they work like a diaper. Same gel core, same job: pull the wetness off her skin so she stays comfortable. She can pull them up herself, which is genuinely useful, but the feeling she needs is still being taken away.

Won’t she just walk around wet?

No. She notices, she tells you, and you change her — usually within a minute or two. The absorbent layer is there to buy you those minutes and keep it off the floor, not to keep her sitting in it.

What if she refuses to wear them?

Let her pick the print, and put them on in the morning before the day gets going rather than mid-standoff. Most refusal at this stage is about being handled, not about the underwear. A child who chose her own pair generally wants to keep them on.

How long does it take?

Days one to three are the messy part. Most parents describe the first “I’m wet” inside the first week or two, and telling-you-before somewhere in the weeks after that. Some children take a couple of weeks and some take a couple of months. You get 75 days precisely because it isn’t instant.

Do we use them at night too?

No. Night dryness is a hormone thing and arrives on its own schedule. Keep the diaper on at night for as long as she needs it.

How many pairs do we need?
