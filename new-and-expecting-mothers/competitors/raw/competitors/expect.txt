## FAQ

Is it safe for me to exercise during my pregnancy?

According to leading global expert on maternal health the American College of Obstetrics and Gynecologists (ACOG): Concerns that regular physical activity during pregnancy may cause miscarriage, poor fetal growth, musculoskeletal injury, or premature delivery have not been substantiated for women with uncomplicated pregnancies. Also, "physical activity and exercise in pregnancy are associated with minimal risks and have been shown to benefit most women, although some modification to exercise routines may be necessary because of normal anatomic and physiologic changes and fetal requirements." It is important that you consult your doctor before you start this or any other fitness program.

How often should I exercise?

The 2018 update to the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans reinforces prior recommendations of at least 150 minutes of moderate intensity aerobic activity per week during pregnancy and the postpartum period 4. This activity should be spread throughout the week. The guidelines advise that women who habitually engaged in vigorous-intensity aerobic activity or who were physically active before pregnancy can continue these activities during pregnancy and the postpartum period. Additionally, women who are pregnant should be under the care of an obstetrician-gynecologist or other obstetric care provider who can monitor the progress of the pregnancy. Women who are pregnant can consult their obstetrician–gynecologist or other obstetric care provider about whether or how to adjust their physical activity during pregnancy and postpartum 4.

‍See complete guidance from the American College of Obstetrics & Gynecologists.

How do I know when I should stop exercising?

The American College of Obstetrics and Gynecologists shares Warning Signs to Discontinue Exercise While Pregnant. If you experience any of these conditions while working out, discontinue exercise, and contact a medical professional:

- Vaginal bleeding

- Abdominal pain

- Regular painful contractions

- Amniotic fluid leakage

- Dyspnea before exertion

- Dizziness

- Headache

- Chest pain

- Muscle weakness affecting balance

- Calf pain or swelling

Are there age guidelines for prenatal fitness?

Research shows that regular exercise may actually reduce some of the risks associated with advanced maternal age. such as pelvic pain and gestational weight gain.

What’s at risk if I don’t exercise during pregnancy and postpartum?

Pregnancy has a similar impact on your body as running a 40-week marathon. You wouldn’t dare run a marathon without training—so why risk pregnancy and postpartum without preparing your body? Research shows that physical inactivity in pregnancy can lead to higher rates of:

- Preterm birth

- Emergency C-section

- Preeclampsia or high blood pressure

- Gestational diabetes

- Type 2 diabetes later in life

- Postpartum depression

Do I need specialized pregnancy fitness?

Probably—pregnancy changes how your body handles exercise.

‍Before launching Expect, we met with the head of the American College of Sports Medicine and asked a simple question: Do pregnant women really need their own fitness program? His answer—grounded in science—shared several key body shifts:

- Balance: Your center of gravity moves forward in pregnancy, boosting fall risk.

- Joints: Hormones loosen ligaments, so certain moves can strain them.

- Blood flow: In the third trimester, lying flat too long can slow blood return and make you dizzy or even faint.

- Endurance: Even elite athletes experience “detraining” during pregnancy—energy drops and pre-baby routines suddenly feel too hard.

‍

That’s why every Expect workout is:

- Timed to each trimester (no risky long-flat positions late in pregnancy)

- Reviewed by our OB/GYN panel for safety and effectiveness

Move with confidence, knowing your workout is built—and medically cleared—for the realities of a pregnant body.
