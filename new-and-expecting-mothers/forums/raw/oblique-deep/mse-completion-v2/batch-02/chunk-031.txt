POST Comment_76585899 | reply | author_id=54666 | 2019-12-08T19:12:25+00:00
https://forums.moneysavingexpert.com/discussion/6076710/early-years-funding-and-bank-holidays#Comment_76585899
To my knowledge the funding is paid term-time only unless you have opted for the 'stretched' option of using the funding all year. I assume you are entitled to the extended hours giving a total of 30 hours each week tto - 1140 hours per year. This works out to approx. 22 hours each week. The funding should be applied to the first 22 hours of your daughters care each week although only a maximum of 10 hours funding can be applied a day. For instance if you have 22 hours each week and her nursery is open 11 hours per day you would pay for 1 hour each Monday and Tuesday and 9 hours on a Wednesday and the full cost on a Thursday.

Surely if they are using the funded hours you are not paying as much out of your own pocket?

Hope that helps

Do you need to reduce it?  Some families would see what you spend as a luxury, others might see it as nowhere near enough.  

We spend around £100 per week for everything, for 2 adults and 2 children, so probably £50 for 1 adult and 1 child, and I think we're pretty thrifty, although there are some things I don't scrimp on like oat milk!

You could try your child with frozen fruits.  I usually find them much cheaper, plus they keep for longer so you don't need to shop as often (which can reduce impulse buying).  We like some frozen fruits - exotic fruits, melon, raspberries, blueberries etc are nice for smoothies or defrosted for on breakfast.  The berries don't have the same texture as fresh ones but we still like them.  We don't like defrosted strawberries though, they are just mushy horribleness.  It's a matter of trying a few things... any you don't like can be used in smoothies or made into crumble.

We use a lot of frozen veg too.  Some people can taste the difference but I find the cheaper price makes up for that, especially if you're cooking recipes rather than just plain boiled veg e.g. with chilli roasted broccoli you might not be able to tell it was frozen, whereas you would definitely be able to if you just steamed or boiled it plain (although I still like it like that!).  

Make sure you're buying the biggest tubs you can use up of things like yoghurt.

Even if the strawberries are mushy, you can take them home, wash them and in freeze, the same for bananas (ensure to peel the skin off and place in freezer bags).

Buy plain yoghurt and add the frozen strawberries, mango and banana

As your baby is growing, protein is extremely important - not sure if you eat chicken, but a small piece for her.

You can also get protein from : lentils, beans (red, black and black eye peas), split peas.  You can use them by making your own veggie burgers - make Google your friend.

I would stay away from soy milk for the baby - do your research on that.

As I said above, I always have frozen fruit in my freezer - mangoes, bananas, strawberries, blue berries etc.

Cooking is an art, but at the same time you are going to have to become creative.

For example, yesterday I made cod fish pie:  black eye peas, chick peas and butter beans sauteed in home made seasoning - place the mixed beans mixture on the bottom - next add smashed potatoes - next the cod fish mixture and finish off the top with smashed potatoes - make for 45 minutes and then place under the broiler to 10 to brown off.

This can be eaten on its own or with a small salad.

This question comes up regularly on this forum.

Usually you'll have a suggestion of a Junior SIPP (it's probably the best financially, but it's a bit boring and they can't access the money when they might need it most, e.g. first car/house/etc), a JISA or childrens savings accounts.

Some (like myself) are a bit more pessimistic on giving an 18 year old unrestricted access to a large sum of money, fearing they will put it up the wall on takeaways, nights out and designer clothing in short order. Unfortunately I have seen it happen first hand in my wider family. Of course we can hope our kids will know better.

Personally I will be investing the money in my own S&S ISA, earmarking some for them, and then making gifts as and when needed (e.g. suprising them by buying their first car).

Young adults aren't renowned for their financial prowess, but others raise a fair point that if they do spaff it up the wall, it teaches them a lesson in the value of money (but I guess it depends on the sum, a couple of hundred might be worth teaching a lesson for, less so for £20k that you scrimped and saved for over the past 18 years).

There is no single right answer.

A bit late to the party but following our first 2 years ago, I will say the thing that caught me most by surprise was the lack of unexpected expenses!

Sure, you'll probably need to dish out a grand for a car seat, pram, cotbed, etc but aside from nappies… and bottles… and formula (if applicable), the costs are quite minimal. Newborn nappies are insanely cheap and you might only be buying a tub of formula a month at the start. Clothes are subjective, sleepsuits and vests are cheap from supermarkets - but it depends on the person. Some parents can go mad and put their babies in designer gear or mountains of clothes they'll never wear.

I will say, the biggest financial impact (though not surprising) is when they go to nursery. We went from spending less than £50 per month on our daughter (outside of one-off costs I mentioned above) to over 500 when she turned 1 and went to nursery - and that's ~3 days per week with '30' free hours.

coupon_underground

I'd be interested to hear what you wish someone had told you before your first child arrived.

Buy used - whatever you're comfortable with. Baby items cost a fortune new and then immediately lose nearly all their value. They're like wedding dresses, no-one wants to be the one buying used for the special occasion.

Except I'd argue it's worse with baby stuff, because aside from 90% of people buying new everything for their precious new family member, oftentimes babies will outgrow clothes/toys/etc before they even use them.

We've picked up countless things (including all the nursery furniture, sterilizer, toys, books, etc) for a fraction of the retail price, oftentimes unused.

The good bit is if you can pick this stuff up used, then use it for a while, you will be able to sell it again for pretty much what you paid for it.

I will say it's extremely easy to spend a lot of money if you're not careful of running on paternal/maternal instinct to buy the best of the best. There's so much gear out there… but babies are simple, they just want feeding, changing, holding and to sleep.
